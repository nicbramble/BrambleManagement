Plan your week with ChatGPT

Turn your task list into three clear outcomes and a realistic week, with five copyable prompts and a worked example.

Plan your week

Five prompts, a realistic schedule, and a quick review.

1. Get everything out of your head2. Pick three outcomes and check capacity3. Give the important work a placeA worked example4. Replan when the week changes5. Review Friday before starting again

A useful weekly plan gives you three clear outcomes, time to work on them, and room for things to change. ChatGPT can help you turn a messy list into that plan in about 15 minutes. You make the decisions; it helps organize the tradeoffs.

Work through the prompts and replace the brackets with your details. ChatGPT only knows information you provide or explicitly connect. Give it your actual commitments. Leave out private details that do not affect the plan.

1. Get everything out of your head

Write down work, errands, appointments, unfinished tasks, and anything you keep remembering at the wrong moment. Messy is fine. Include your working hours, fixed commitments, deadlines, and when your energy tends to be highest.

Distinguish a real deadline from a date you would simply prefer. “Send the client proposal by Thursday at noon” is useful; “catch up on business” needs a clearer next step.

Copy, customize, practice
Help me plan the week of [date] in [time zone].
My brain dump: [tasks, projects, errands, and worries].
Working hours: [days and times].
Fixed commitments, including travel and breaks: [days and times].
Confirmed deadlines: [task, date, and time].
Energy patterns: [best focus periods and lower-energy periods].

Organize this into fixed commitments, deadline-driven work, flexible tasks, and items to clarify. Keep preferred dates separate from confirmed deadlines. Identify dependencies and vague tasks. Ask up to three questions that would most change the plan. Do not invent commitments or deadlines. Wait for my answers before scheduling.

2. Pick three outcomes and check capacity

Choose what you want to be true by Friday. An outcome describes a finished result: send the proposal, publish two videos, or submit the expense claim. “Work on content” gives you less help deciding what to do next.

Three is a useful limit. A difficult week might have one main outcome. Put other tasks on a “later” list so they stay visible without competing for every open hour.

Calculate capacity from usable time. Subtract meetings, breaks, recurring admin, and personal commitments first. Keep roughly 20–30% of the remaining time free as a starting buffer, then adjust using what past weeks have actually required.

Copy, customize, practice
Using my answers, propose up to three outcomes for this week. Explain each choice using deadlines, impact, and dependencies. Define what done means and list the next concrete actions.

My usable work time after fixed commitments, breaks, and recurring admin is [hours]. Reserve [percentage]% as unscheduled buffer. Show the arithmetic and the maximum hours available for planned tasks.

My task estimates are [task and hours, or unknown]. Use my estimates where available; label any suggested estimates as assumptions. If the work does not fit, recommend what to reduce, defer, or discuss with the person expecting it. Do not compress everything to make it appear possible. Ask me to approve the outcomes and estimates before scheduling.

3. Give the important work a place

Put demanding tasks in your best focus periods and group short admin tasks together. Break large outcomes into concrete blocks, such as “draft the proposal outline.” Include preparation, checking, and delivery time.

Copy, customize, practice
Build a Monday–Friday plan from the outcomes and estimates I approved. Respect all fixed commitments, deadlines, breaks, and energy preferences. Keep the agreed buffer unscheduled.

For each work block, give the day, start and end time, specific action, and expected result. Complete dependent steps in order and leave time to check and deliver work before its deadline. Show total planned hours and remaining capacity. Flag conflicts or missing information rather than silently changing commitments. Finish with the first action to take on Monday.

Check the draft yourself. Confirm deadlines, time estimates, travel, overlapping blocks, and any preparation the plan missed. Add the approved blocks to your calendar manually. A suggested schedule in this chat does not mean calendar events or reminders have been created.

A worked example

Imagine you have 25 usable hours after meetings, breaks, and recurring admin. Your fixed meetings include Tuesday 10–12 and Thursday 2–4. You focus best in the morning.

Keeping 20% free reserves five hours, leaving 20 hours available for planned tasks. You choose:

  1. Send a client proposal by Thursday noon: eight hours.
  2. Finish two short videos by Friday: six hours.
  3. Submit receipts by Wednesday evening: two hours.

That is 16 hours of work. You have four more hours of planning capacity plus the five-hour buffer. You can leave both available; open time does not need a task.

  • Monday: 9–11, proposal research; 11:30–12:30, collect receipts.
  • Tuesday: 1–3, draft the proposal; 3:30–4:30, submit receipts.
  • Wednesday: 9–12, complete the proposal draft; 1–3, outline the videos.
  • Thursday: 9–10, check and send the proposal; 11–1, film the videos.
  • Friday: 9–11, edit and finish the videos.

The plan avoids the fixed meetings and sends the proposal before its deadline. If your real filming takes four hours, change that estimate before approving the week.

4. Replan when the week changes

Check on Wednesday, or as soon as a new commitment changes your capacity. Update the remaining work, not just the original task list. A task that is half finished may still have most of its difficult work ahead.

Copy, customize, practice
Update my weekly plan using this status:
Completed: [results].
Remaining work and revised estimates: [tasks and hours].
New commitments or deadlines: [details].
Usable time left: [hours and available blocks].

Keep confirmed deadlines and fixed commitments intact. Recalculate capacity with [percentage]% buffer. Propose a revised schedule and explicitly name what moves, shrinks, or drops. If a deadline cannot fit, say so and suggest the decision or conversation I need to have. Give me one next action.

5. Review Friday before starting again

Take five minutes to compare the plan with what happened. Look for information you can use: tasks that took longer, focus periods that worked, and interruptions that deserve a larger buffer. Carry forward unfinished work deliberately instead of copying the entire list.

Copy, customize, practice
Review this week with me.
Planned outcomes: [list].
Actual results: [list].
Estimated versus actual time: [details].
Interruptions and energy notes: [details].

Identify what worked, where estimates were off, and one change to try next week. Recommend what to carry forward, defer, or remove, with reasons. Create a short planning note I can paste into next week's chat, including unfinished work, confirmed deadlines, and revised estimates. Ask me to confirm missing information rather than guessing.

Save that note alongside your calendar. Next week, start with what you learned and the commitments you already know. A useful plan gets easier to follow when it reflects your actual week.
